# Summary
This project studies how community-level public health and socioeconomic conditions relate to gun violence victimization across Chicago community areas. Our motivation came from two directions. First, Chicago publishes both a historical community-area public-health dataset and a violence-reduction victimization dataset through the City of Chicago Data Portal. Second, those two datasets can be linked through a shared geographic unit, the official Chicago community area. That combination made the project a good fit for a data curation workflow because the work is not only about statistical analysis; it also requires acquisition, cleaning, standardization, integration, validation, and careful documentation. We wanted the final repository to show a reproducible path from raw public data to interpretable results, rather than a notebook that only works after manual preparation.

Our main research question is: which community-level public health and socioeconomic indicators are most strongly associated with higher gun violence victimization burden across Chicago community areas? We also asked several secondary questions. How unevenly is victimization burden distributed across community areas? Which areas appear most burdened when we summarize a fixed study window? Do poverty, unemployment, income, and teen birth rate move in directions that match the descriptive patterns suggested by public-health literature and prior urban inequality research? Finally, can a lightweight and interpretable regression summarize how those indicators relate to mean annual gun violence burden without overstating causality?

The final workflow integrates two public datasets. The first is the historical community-area public-health dataset, which contains 77 rows and 29 columns of health and socioeconomic indicators. The second is the victim-level violence dataset, which contained 63,245 rows at acquisition time. For the final submission, we restricted the violence data to the 2005-2010 study window and then cleaned and aggregated it into a community-year panel. That decision was necessary for two reasons. The legacy notebook and status report contained contradictory time-window claims, and the final project needed one defensible, documented study frame. In addition, the project required a workflow that could be rerun from raw files in a clean environment, so all assumptions had to be made explicit and encoded in scripts.

The curation work turned out to matter as much as the analysis. During profiling and integration we identified several issues that would have weakened the submission if left unresolved: the raw violence data still exposed homicide victim name fields, the source used the spelling variant `MONTCLARE` while the public-health crosswalk uses `MONTCLAIRE`, `O'HARE` appeared in alternate forms, and the violence data contained only 76 observed community areas in the study window because Edison Park had zero incidents. A naive merge therefore dropped valid geography. The final pipeline fixes the name mismatches, removes direct identifiers from all processed outputs, and zero-fills the complete 77-community by 6-year panel so the integrated tables are structurally complete.

Our findings are descriptive and associational rather than causal. The strongest positive simple correlation between mean annual gun violence victim counts and the selected indicators was `teen_birth_rate` at approximately `0.719`. `Below_poverty_level` and `unemployment` were also positively correlated with violence burden at about `0.481` and `0.466`, while `per_capita_income` was negatively correlated at about `-0.399`. The top community areas by mean annual total victims in the study window were Austin, Humboldt Park, West Englewood, Roseland, and Englewood. In the lightweight regression model, the four selected predictors explained about `68%` of the variation in `log1p(mean_annual_total_victims)` across community areas (`R^2 = 0.680`). We interpret those results cautiously. The public-health dataset is static by community area, the violence source changes over time, and nonfatal shooting coverage in this source is concentrated in 2010. The project therefore supports careful community-level pattern description, not causal claims about why violence occurs.

# Data profile
We use two complementary datasets from the City of Chicago Data Portal, both of which are stored in `data/raw/` and can also be reacquired programmatically by the workflow. The public-health dataset is stored at `data/raw/Public_Health_Statistics_-_Selected_public_health_indicators_by_Chicago_community_area_-_Historical_20260302.csv`. It contains one row for each of Chicago’s 77 official community areas and 29 columns overall. The key identifier fields are `Community Area` and `Community Area Name`. The remaining columns describe public-health and socioeconomic characteristics such as birth rate, teen birth rate, poverty, unemployment, per-capita income, diabetes-related outcomes, firearm-related outcomes, lead screening, and education-related measures. Structurally, this is a relatively compact, wide table with one geographic observation per row and a mix of rates and counts already summarized by the source agency. For our project, it plays two roles. First, it supplies the socioeconomic indicators needed for the main research question. Second, it acts as the authoritative crosswalk for official community-area names and numbers.

The violence dataset is stored at `data/raw/Violence_Reduction_-_Victims_of_Homicides_and_Non-Fatal_Shootings_20260302.csv`. At acquisition time it contained 63,245 victim-level records and 38 columns. Important fields include `DATE`, `COMMUNITY_AREA`, `VICTIMIZATION_PRIMARY`, `INCIDENT_PRIMARY`, `GUNSHOT_INJURY_I`, `AGE`, `SEX`, and `RACE`. Because the unit of observation is a victimization record rather than a community-year summary, this table needed much more transformation before it could be joined to the public-health indicators. It also includes direct homicide victim name fields in the raw source, which makes it especially important to distinguish raw files from publishable processed outputs. In our final workflow, those identifier columns remain only in the raw acquisition file and are removed from all downstream artifacts.

The two datasets relate to our research question through a shared spatial unit: Chicago community area. That linkage is conceptually appropriate because the project is about area-level burden rather than individual-level risk. However, the relationship between the two sources is not trivial. The public-health indicators are static by community area, while the violence records are time stamped and event based. For that reason, the integrated dataset cannot be interpreted as a strict longitudinal panel of jointly observed annual covariates and outcomes. Instead, we use the public-health file as a set of community characteristics and the violence file as the annual outcome source. We then create two final products: a `community_year_panel.csv` for 2005-2010 victim counts and a `community_level_analysis.csv` that joins community-level average annual burden to the static indicators.

The key raw data quality and integration characteristics are visible in the repository structure. Cleaned health indicators are saved to `data/interim/health_indicators_clean.csv`, cleaned violence records are saved to `data/interim/violence_records_clean.csv`, the zero-filled community-year panel is saved to `data/processed/community_year_panel.csv`, and the final community-level table is saved to `data/processed/community_level_analysis.csv`. Supporting outputs from the quality assessment and workflow validation are saved under `results/tables/` and `metadata/`.

Ethically, the violence dataset is the more sensitive source. Even though it is published by a public data portal, victim-level public safety data still creates privacy and harm risks if republished carelessly. We therefore remove direct identifiers, analyze only aggregated outputs, and do not make individual-level claims about people or communities. We also avoid stigmatizing language in the report. Legally, the team-created code and documentation in this repository are released under the MIT License, but that license does not apply to the two source datasets. Both datasets come from the City of Chicago Data Portal and remain governed by the source portal’s terms of use. In our review of the dataset pages, we did not find a clearly stated dataset-specific license for either source. Because these are governmental open-data resources, we treat them cautiously as public data intended for open use, while explicitly noting that this is an inference rather than a portal-stated license label. For that reason, we preserve attribution, cite the original dataset pages, retain provenance, and do not attempt to relicense the city data under MIT. Because both files are under GitHub’s size threshold for this project, we include them directly in the repository. If the raw CSVs are deleted during grading, the acquisition script recreates them from the official portal URLs.

# Data quality
The quality assessment in this project was not treated as a single checklist item; it drove several core design decisions in the final workflow. We profiled both raw datasets before cleaning and saved the quantitative summary to `results/tables/raw_quality_metrics.csv` and the machine-readable issue summary to `results/tables/raw_quality_findings.json`. The raw metrics show that the health dataset has `77` rows and `29` columns, while the violence dataset has `63,245` rows and `38` columns. The profiling step also confirmed four concrete issues that had to be addressed before the repository could be considered reproducible and curation-complete.

The first issue was direct identifiers in the raw violence data. The file includes three homicide victim name fields: first name, middle initial, and last name. Leaving those fields in any processed or analysis-ready artifact would be a privacy and responsible-use failure. For a milestone notebook this risk can be overlooked because the focus tends to drift toward plots, but for MS4 the pipeline itself needed to enforce the privacy boundary. Our validation step therefore checks that no publishable processed output contains any of those name columns.

The second issue was inconsistency in the geographic key. The public-health file contains the official 77 community areas with official names and numbers, but the violence file uses community-area names in free-text form. During integration we found at least two concrete inconsistencies that matter: `MONTCLARE` appears in the violence source whereas the official community name in the health file is `MONTCLAIRE`, and `OHARE` can appear without the apostrophe used in the official `O'HARE` spelling. Without explicit standardization rules, those records fail to match the crosswalk cleanly. That makes the join fragile and can silently drop valid rows.

The third issue was incomplete community coverage in the raw violence study window. When we profiled the violence source from 2005 through 2010, it covered only `76` community areas because Edison Park had zero incidents in that window. A simple inner join or naive aggregation would therefore make the city appear to have only 76 areas, which is incorrect. This is a classic data-quality problem at the integration stage: the source is not “wrong,” but the workflow is wrong if it treats missing observations as nonexistent geography. The final `community_year_panel.csv` solves this by building the full `77 x 6 = 462` community-year grid first and then filling absent counts with zeros. The corresponding integration checks saved to `results/tables/integration_checks.csv` confirm that the final panel has 77 communities, 6 years, and 462 rows, while Edison Park’s total burden is preserved as zero rather than omitted.

The fourth issue was field coercion and temporal interpretation. The health dataset uses `.` placeholders in some numeric fields, which would break quantitative analysis if read as literal strings. We counted `12` such placeholder cells and converted them to missing values during cleaning. The violence source also exposed a documentation inconsistency inherited from the earlier milestone work: older narrative sections referred to a `2005-2011` overlap, but the integrated artifact actually covered `2005-2010`. The MS4 workflow resolves this by freezing one explicit study window and making the scripts, validation checks, and README consistent with that choice.

There are also substantive data-quality limitations that remain even after cleaning. The public-health dataset is static rather than annual, so the project compares annualized violence burden to community characteristics that may reflect a broader historical period. The violence source is updated over time, and in the final cleaned data the nonfatal shooting component appears only in 2010 within the selected study window. That means the total-victim trend is not equally comparable across all years for both subcomponents. We treat that as a documented limitation rather than something that can be “fixed” computationally. In short, the final repository addresses structural quality problems that are under our control and documents source limitations that are not.

# Data cleaning
The data-cleaning workflow is implemented in `scripts/clean_data.py`, with integration logic in `scripts/integrate_data.py`. Cleaning begins by standardizing raw column names into snake_case so the downstream code can use a consistent schema. In the public-health file, `Community Area` becomes `community_area_number` and `Community Area Name` becomes `community_area_name`, while all indicator columns are coerced to numeric values after replacing `.` placeholders with missing values. This step addresses type inconsistency and makes the health file safe to use for analysis and joins.

The violence cleaning step is more involved. We first parse the raw `DATE` field into a proper timestamp, derive `year`, and restrict the dataset to the final study window of 2005-2010. We then standardize the community-area names, applying explicit normalization rules for values such as `MONTCLARE` and `OHARE`. Next, we construct the project’s outcome flags. A record is counted as a homicide victim when `victimization_primary == HOMICIDE`. A record is counted as a nonfatal shooting victim when it is not a homicide but has `gunshot_injury_i == YES`. The combined gun violence victim indicator is the union of those two categories. This produces a cleaned victim-level table that is aligned with the project question rather than the broader source file.

Privacy protection is built directly into the cleaning stage. The three homicide victim name fields are dropped before any cleaned dataset is written. The cleaned violence output therefore retains the analytic fields we need, such as age, sex, race, incident timing, and community area, while removing direct identifiers that should not appear in publishable processed files. This is one of the most important cleaning operations in the project because it addresses an ethical and reproducibility issue at the same time: the final pipeline cannot depend on a human remembering to manually delete sensitive columns.

Integration then uses the cleaned health table as the official community crosswalk. Cleaned violence records are matched onto the crosswalk by standardized community-area name to recover `community_area_number`, after which all downstream aggregation is anchored on the official numeric identifier. We aggregate the cleaned violence records by year and community area to produce homicide, nonfatal shooting, and total gun violence victim counts. The workflow then constructs the full community-year grid for all 77 community areas across 2005-2010 and left-joins the observed counts onto that grid. Missing counts are filled with zero. This directly addresses the dropped-community problem detected during profiling and ensures that communities such as Edison Park remain present even when the study window contains no events.

Finally, we derive the community-level analysis table by averaging annual counts over the six-year window and joining those averages back to the static public-health indicators. This produces a clean table for correlation analysis and the lightweight regression model. The resulting outputs are easier to interpret, free of direct identifiers, and structurally complete for the study design.

# Findings
The final outputs show a strong geographic concentration of gun violence victimization burden across Chicago community areas. In `results/tables/top_communities.csv`, the ten highest-burden communities by mean annual total victims from 2005 through 2010 are Austin (`65.83`), Humboldt Park (`46.33`), West Englewood (`42.00`), Roseland (`37.83`), Englewood (`37.17`), Greater Grand Crossing (`36.67`), North Lawndale (`34.00`), Chicago Lawn (`33.83`), Auburn Gresham (`33.33`), and South Shore (`33.00`). That ranking confirms that the burden is not evenly distributed across the city and that a relatively small set of community areas carries much higher average victim counts than many others.

The citywide yearly totals in `results/figures/annual_totals.png` show that homicide victims remain substantial throughout the full 2005-2010 window, while nonfatal shooting victims appear only in 2010 within this cleaned study frame. The final community-year panel sums to 446 homicide victims in 2005, 474 in 2006, 448 in 2007, 517 in 2008, 461 in 2009, and 439 in 2010. Because nonfatal shooting victims contribute 2,445 records in 2010 and zero in the earlier years of the cleaned panel, that component should be interpreted as a source-coverage feature rather than a true absence of nonfatal shootings before 2010. The total 2010 burden is therefore much larger than the preceding years and should not be read as a simple year-over-year jump in comparable measurement.

At the community level, the descriptive correlations align with the broad motivation for the project. `results/tables/correlations.csv` shows that `teen_birth_rate` has the strongest positive Pearson correlation with `mean_annual_total_victims` at approximately `0.719`. `Below_poverty_level` and `unemployment` are also positively correlated with mean annual total victims at about `0.481` and `0.466`, while `per_capita_income` is negatively correlated at about `-0.399`. In plain terms, communities with higher poverty, unemployment, and teen birth rate tended to have higher observed victimization burden in this study window, while communities with higher per-capita income tended to have lower burden.

The regression model in `results/models/linear_model_metrics.json` provides a compact multivariable summary rather than a causal explanation. Using `log1p(mean_annual_total_victims)` as the outcome and the four predeclared predictors, the model achieved `R^2 = 0.680` and adjusted `R^2 = 0.662` across all 77 communities. `Teen_birth_rate` remained highly statistically significant, while `below_poverty_level` and `unemployment` also showed significant coefficients. The unemployment coefficient turns negative in the multivariable model even though its simple correlation is positive, which likely reflects overlap among the predictors rather than a substantive claim that unemployment reduces violence burden. We therefore treat the regression as a descriptive summary of joint association, not as evidence of causal direction.

Overall, the findings support the original project question in a careful and limited sense: community areas with more socioeconomic stress indicators also tend to have higher observed gun violence victimization burden in this integrated dataset. The strongest contribution of the project, however, is not a novel claim about Chicago. It is the curated, reproducible pipeline that makes those descriptive findings transparent, inspectable, and rerunnable.

# Future work
The clearest lesson from this project is that future work should extend the curation foundation rather than just add more modeling. The current pipeline successfully acquires, cleans, integrates, validates, and analyzes the two selected sources, but the analysis is constrained by the structure of those sources. The most important next step would be to add a population denominator dataset by community area so that the project can compare rates rather than only victim counts. Counts are easy to interpret, but they still reflect community size. A rate-based extension would make comparisons across community areas more defensible.

Another useful extension would be a better treatment of time. The current public-health dataset is static by community area, which means the workflow compares annual violence outcomes to characteristics that do not vary by year in the analysis table. If a time-varying health or socioeconomic dataset were available at the same geographic grain, the project could construct a true aligned panel and ask stronger questions about change over time. Even then, causal interpretation would still require caution, but the analytical design would be richer than the current static-covariate setup.

The violence source itself also motivates future work. In the cleaned study window, the nonfatal shooting component appears only in 2010. A future version of the project should investigate source coverage more deeply and either choose a study window that better matches the availability of both victim types or explicitly split the analysis into homicide-only and broader gunshot victimization sections. That would reduce the risk of readers overinterpreting time trends that are partly driven by source structure rather than substantive change.

From a workflow standpoint, there are several improvements worth making if this project were developed beyond the class. The repository could add formal automated tests for each script, a continuous integration job that reruns the workflow on every push, and a release automation step that writes the final tag and checksum manifest automatically. A more ambitious version could also package the analysis in a Docker image or a fully pinned conda environment to reduce cross-platform dependency drift. For the course, `requirements.txt`, `pip-freeze.txt`, and the Snakemake pipeline are sufficient, but a research-grade publication workflow would benefit from stronger environment encapsulation.

There is also room to improve documentation and metadata. The repository now includes a schema.org JSON-LD metadata file, a citation file, checksums, and a codebook, which is a substantial improvement over the milestone state. Future work could expand that metadata into a fuller DCAT or DataCite record, register an archived release through Zenodo, and publish a DOI-backed snapshot of both the code and non-restricted outputs. That would improve FAIR alignment and make the project easier for others to reuse outside the course context.

Finally, the analytical side could be broadened carefully. Spatial smoothing, uncertainty intervals, clustered comparisons, and sensitivity analyses around alternative outcome definitions would all be reasonable follow-ups. The main caution is that each analytical extension should remain subordinate to the same data curation principles that guided MS4: explicit assumptions, reproducible computation, privacy-aware outputs, and no unsupported causal claims.

# Challenges
The main challenge in this project was not finding something interesting to plot; it was converting a milestone notebook into a final submission that could survive grading in a clean environment. Earlier work in the repository lived mostly in a notebook-centered structure with derived CSVs already present. That is convenient for local iteration, but it is risky for MS4 because the TA explicitly warned that graders may remove input and output CSVs and rerun the workflow from scratch. The transition from “working notebook” to “working repository” required us to rethink the project as a pipeline.

A second challenge was integration quality. The two source datasets do not share a perfect, ready-made join key. The public-health source uses official community area names and numbers, while the violence source uses community names in text form. It was easy to miss that `MONTCLARE` and `MONTCLAIRE` refer to the same place, or that `OHARE` must map to `O'HARE`. The earlier merged artifact also silently dropped Edison Park because there were no incidents in the study window, which created the false impression that the city had only 76 relevant community areas. Identifying and correcting those issues took careful profiling and explicit validation checks, not just another merge command.

Privacy was another challenge. The raw violence dataset includes homicide victim name fields, and those columns were still present in the earlier milestone-era merged output. That created an immediate tension between analysis convenience and responsible handling of sensitive data. The final solution was to formalize the distinction between raw and processed data, remove identifiers in the cleaning stage, and validate that no downstream outputs reintroduce them. This was important not only for ethics but also for repository quality: a reproducible workflow should encode the correct privacy behavior instead of depending on manual cleanup.

A fourth challenge was methodological rather than technical. Once the final workflow was rebuilt, the cleaned results made it clear that nonfatal shooting records only appear in 2010 within the selected study window. That affects how the annual trends can be interpreted and limits the comparability of the total-victim series over time. We could have hidden that issue by keeping vague milestone language, but that would have made the final report less honest. Instead, we standardized the study window, documented the limitation, and framed the findings as descriptive.

The final challenge was coordination and visible contribution. Course grading explicitly depends on clear participation from all team members, and notebook-heavy repositories can make contributions look uneven even when the labor was shared. We addressed that in two ways: by dividing responsibilities more explicitly between workflow curation and narrative/analysis presentation, and by writing a detailed contribution statement in this README. The final repository therefore reflects both the technical work of curation and the collaborative work needed to make the submission understandable and reproducible.

# Reproducing
Tested environment:
- Python `3.13.5`
- Primary workflow command: `bash run_all.sh`
- Direct Snakemake command: `snakemake --cores 1 all`

Install dependencies in a clean environment:
1. Create and activate a virtual environment.
2. Install the pinned requirements with `python3 -m pip install -r requirements.txt`.
3. Confirm `snakemake --version` works.

Run the workflow:
1. From the repository root, run `bash run_all.sh`.
2. The wrapper creates local cache directories and then runs Snakemake.
3. If the raw CSVs in `data/raw/` are missing, `scripts/acquire_data.py` downloads them from the official City of Chicago endpoints before the rest of the workflow runs.
4. The workflow then generates checksums, raw-data profiling outputs, cleaned intermediate tables, processed analysis tables, figures, model summaries, the results manifest, and the validation report.

Expected key outputs:
- `metadata/checksums.sha256`
- `data/interim/health_indicators_clean.csv`
- `data/interim/violence_records_clean.csv`
- `data/processed/community_year_panel.csv`
- `data/processed/community_level_analysis.csv`
- `results/figures/annual_totals.png`
- `results/figures/top_10_communities.png`
- `results/figures/indicator_relationships.png`
- `results/models/linear_model_metrics.json`
- `metadata/validation_report.json`

Notes for graders and collaborators:
- No API key is required. The City of Chicago Socrata endpoints used by this project are public.
- An optional `SOCRATA_APP_TOKEN` environment variable can be set for rate limiting, but the workflow does not require it.
- To verify reproducibility from a near-clean state, delete generated outputs under `data/interim/`, `data/processed/`, `results/`, and `metadata/checksums.sha256`, then rerun `bash run_all.sh`.
- The archived milestone notebook and earlier milestone reports are preserved under `archive/` for provenance, but they are not required to reproduce the final outputs.

Repository guide:
- `scripts/`: stage-specific Python scripts for acquisition, profiling, cleaning, integration, analysis, and validation.
- `Snakefile`: workflow orchestration.
- `data/raw/`: raw source files.
- `data/interim/`: cleaned intermediate data.
- `data/processed/`: final analysis-ready tables.
- `results/`: figures, tables, model outputs, and manifest.
- `docs/data_dictionary.md`: codebook for the project datasets.
- `metadata/project.jsonld`: machine-readable project metadata.
